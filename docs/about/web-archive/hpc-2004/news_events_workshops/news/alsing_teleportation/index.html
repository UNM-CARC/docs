The Sante Fe New Mexican Newspaper Article

Monday, November 17, 2003

UNM Physicist Sheds Light on Teleportation

By JEFF TOLLEFSON | The New Mexican


Star Trekkers beware: Teleportation might fail if used during warp drive.

Well, that might be stretching it, but acceleration could interfere with teleportation at the quantum level, according to a recent paper in Physical Review Letters co-authored by University of New Mexico physicist Paul Alsing.

Researchers several years ago demonstrated that some kind of teleportation works in the weird world of quantum mechanics. The trick lies with particle "entanglement," a state in which to particles in a sense become one, even if they are sent to opposite sides of a room or, theoretically, the universe.

By measuring, and necessarily destroying, one entangled particle, "Alice" can transport its exact properties to the second particle, to be decoded by "Bob" (both of whom are frequent characters in physics papers).

This is where Alsing's paper comes in. He looked at what would happen if Bob were accelerating with his particle. Without getting into the messy details, the accelerating particle necessarily moves through a "thermal bath" of particles created by the acceleration. These particles would interfere with Bob's ability to measure and decode his particle.

What has this to do with anything? It might be a far cry from any human experience, but Alsing says it serves as a window into how quantum mechanics might interact with general relativity, two great theories of the 20th century that scientists desperately wish to reconcile with each other. The problem is that general relativity, while accurate at predicting the effects of gravity on light and planetary movements, breaks down at the quantum level.

In this case, acceleration serves as a proxy for gravity, and thus general relativity, according to Alsing. He says the scenario is similar to one in which Alice is falling into a black hole and Bob is accelerating at the outer edge to escape gravity and keep from falling in.

"It's not general relativity, but it's a sort of baby step toward that," he said. "This is essentially what Stephen Hawking did to get Hawking radiation," the flow of particles believed to emanate from the fringe of a black hole.

Spooky theoretical physics aside, Alsing's prediction isn't likely to affect anything on earth. Consequently, if quantum computing ever gets off the ground, teleportation might yet be a means of moving information.
